# Operation Toofan — Anti-Narcotics Drive (Keralam)
{: .no_toc }

{% include last_updated.html %}

## Summary

Operation Toofan is the UDF government's statewide anti-narcotics drive,
launched June 2, 2026 and now in its second phase ("Toofan 2.0," from
September 28). The launch and headline results are tracked as
[entry #3](achievements/2026.html#operation-toofan-launch) and
[entry #6](achievements/2026.html#operation-toofan-2) in UDF Government
Achievements; one arrest arising from it is tracked as a controversy in
[entry #16](controversies/2026.html#toofan-mdma-bust) of UDF
Government Controversies. This page is a separate, cross-checked look at
recurring criticism of the
operation's design and execution — privacy, treatment capacity, targeting,
and how enforcement and rehabilitation are actually being weighted against
each other — rather than a chronological log of events.

## Notes

### Privacy and public exposure


<div class="entry-card" markdown="1">

#### 1. Media visibility and identity protection for people who use drugs
{: #media-visibility }

*Ongoing since June 2026*

The operation is deliberately social-media-driven — Kerala Police posts
raid, seizure, and awareness videos, and runs public tip-off lines — which
has amplified some episodes far beyond police control. In one case, a
local Instagram account's reel of alleged public drug use in Perumbavoor
reached, per ThePrint, more than 2 million views by the next morning after
prominent accounts tagged Kerala Police's page; Kerala Kaumudi independently
confirms the reel, the tagging and two arrests, but gives no view count, so
the 2 million figure rests on ThePrint alone. Separately, a July 3 Sabrang
India analysis of Perumbavoor coverage argued that police inspections in
migrant settlements and labour camps were being filmed and circulated
widely, with the filming itself framed as "civic responsibility" —
raising concerns about the dignity of a group that is economically
essential but socially peripheral and poorly placed to contest how it's
portrayed. That analysis is the only source found for this specific point. No reporting was found of a specific policy proposal to keep
referred users' identities confidential (e.g. anonymized rehab referral) —
that appears to be a real gap rather than a live policy debate.

### Awareness and rehabilitation capacity


<div class="entry-card" markdown="1">

#### 2. Awareness programme: activity claims vs. measured effectiveness

*Ongoing since June 2026*

The government's own figures for the "Toofan Warriors" awareness wing are
large — 10,295 awareness classes as of September 26 (a police/government
figure), alongside a claimed 20–30% reduction in drug use and peddling
(Chennithala's words as reported by PTI; some headlines round it to a flat
30%). No outlet was found testing that claim against data. The News
Minute's reporting frames the operation overall as one that is "yielding
results from an enforcement perspective but fails to address drug abuse as
a pressing public health concern" — i.e. the criticism is less that no
awareness activity happened, and more that high activity counts aren't
shown to translate into treatment uptake or behaviour change (that framing
is The News Minute's, not independently corroborated). For
context only — this is a **different program under the previous LDF
government**, not Operation Toofan — a 2022 anti-drug campaign drew a
structurally similar critique from a Kerala State Mental Health Authority
psychiatrist, who said no mental health professionals, social scientists,
or sociologists were consulted in designing it. Flagging it here only
because the same design gap (awareness campaigns built without mental
health expertise) recurs across governments, not because it's evidence
about Toofan itself.

#### 3. Toofan Care: treatment capacity vs. arrest volume
{: #toofan-care-capacity }

*Ongoing since June 2026*

Toofan Care, the rehabilitation arm, involves around 35 hospitals (a Home
Department/government claim repeated by ThePrint/PTI, Daijiworld and The
Tribune). On September 28, Home Minister Chennithala said more than 100
people had been admitted to hospitals under it — a government figure that couldn't be verified independently — while the same round of PTI reporting put
arrests at 12,584 as of September 26 (earlier police figures: 11,776
arrests in 10,918 cases as of September 17, per Asianet, Times Kerala and
The News Minute). That isn't a like-for-like ratio, since not everyone
arrested is dependent, but it shows how small treatment throughput is next
to enforcement volume. Addiction psychiatrist Dr. Mohan Roy, quoted by The
News Minute (the only outlet found quoting him), says it is "not always
clear what substance the particular arrested user has consumed," which
blocks proper treatment planning.

#### 4. Custody and hospital access for arrested users
{: #custody-access }

*Ongoing since June 2026*

The News Minute reports that the Toofan Care hospital tie-up doesn't
appear to extend to people arrested for possessing small quantities — it
found no dedicated system to assess their treatment needs, with users
remanded, released on bail and often returning to drug use. (The
government describes Toofan Care as helpline-driven, with police
coordinating with hospitals, and no source says arrestees are excluded;
this is The News Minute's reading.) The same report quotes unnamed jail
and police officials describing withdrawal-related violence — one jail
superintendent said inmates "would turn violent due to withdrawal
symptoms," needing isolation and sedation for days — and quotes Dr. Mohan
Roy recommending that such detainees be treated inside jails with proper
facilities, and that police, excise and prison staff be trained. All of
that is The News Minute alone. A separate incident at Maradu police
station in Kochi in mid-September, where an MDMA-possession accused banged
his head against cell walls and injured officers, is independently
reported by Onmanorama, ThePrint and Kerala Kaumudi, but only The News
Minute connects it to withdrawal.

### Enforcement targeting


<div class="entry-card" markdown="1">

#### 5. Peddlers vs. users: who is actually being arrested
{: #peddlers-vs-users }

*Ongoing since June 2026*

The News Minute reports that through September 1, 2026 police had
registered 3,127 small-quantity (personal-use) cases against 116
commercial-quantity (trafficking) cases — about 27 to 1. This figure is
single-sourced, and another count differs: a PTI report on August 31,
quoting nodal officer IG Putta Vimaladithya, gave 165 commercial-quantity
and 395 intermediate-quantity cases. It also isn't a full breakdown: total
registered cases were about 9,981 by September 4 (Konni Vartha), so the
3,127 small-quantity cases are roughly a third of all cases, and no breakdown of the rest was found. What the figure supports is that small-quantity
cases far outnumber commercial-quantity ones — a concrete version of the
"target peddlers, not users" concern — but not that arrests are
"dominated" by possession cases overall. The operation's stated goal, per
its June 2026 launch framing, was to "overhaul the drug mafia," not simply
arrest users or low-level dealers.

#### 6. Official rhetoric on reform vs. the execution gap
{: #rhetoric-vs-execution }

*September 2026*

The government's own framing already lines up with "reform, not shame."
As Toofan 2.0 launched, Home Minister Ramesh Chennithala told PTI the
campaign has three parts — in the wording PTI carried: "One is the police
taking action and putting the offenders in jail. Number two is to find
(those affected). Number three is to provide care. We want to rehabilitate
the people who were actually using drugs." (The Tribune renders it slightly
differently, so treat the quote as approximate; "three-pronged" and "reform,
not shame" are this page's framing, not his.) The gap isn't in the stated policy; it's that this
distinction doesn't yet show up cleanly in outcomes (see entries
[#3](#toofan-care-capacity)–[#5](#peddlers-vs-users) above) — a say–do gap rather than the government rejecting a
reform-over-punishment framing outright.

### Underlying mental health context


<div class="entry-card" markdown="1">

#### 7. Mental health access as a possible driver — plausible, not yet documented for Toofan specifically

*Background context*

{: .note }
Unlike the entries above, no report found draws a direct, explicit line
from Kerala's mental health care gap to Operation Toofan's arrest
population, or names specific professions like engineers or doctors as an
at-risk group in that context. This entry lays out the underlying data
that makes the theory plausible, not a confirmed causal claim.

Kerala has only 5.53 mental health beds per 10,000 people (2018 data),
against a rising burden: mental illness prevalence rose from 194 to 300
per 100,000 people between 2002 and 2018, and the National Mental Health
Survey (2015–16) recorded an 11.36% overall prevalence of mental
disorders in the state, with substance use disorders at 4.82%. A
Kerala-specific study of 400 IT professionals working from home (surveyed
Dec 2022–Mar 2023) found 24% anxiety and 11% stress prevalence, with
substance abuse — alongside night shifts and inadequate rest —
significantly linked to poor mental health outcomes in that group. More
broadly (and predating Toofan — this data is from a 2025 report, under
the previous government), the beneficiary count for de-addiction services
in Kerala rose from 4,239 in 2020–21 to 10,385 in 2022–23, with one
Kochi-based source describing drug use as having "spread across all
sectors, from IT professionals to school kids." None of this is
Toofan-specific evidence; it's the background rate at which Kerala's
mental health and addiction-services gap appears to be biting across
professional classes, which is the closest verifiable support for the
"people turn to drugs for relief where mental health care is
inaccessible" hypothesis.

**Sources:**
- [Is Kerala's mental health burden outpacing its care capacity? (The South First)](https://thesouthfirst.com/health/is-keralas-mental-health-burden-outpacing-its-care-capacity/)
- [Prevalence and determinants of depression, anxiety, and stress among IT professionals working from home (International Journal of Occupational Safety and Health)](https://nepjol.info/index.php/IJOSH/article/view/72275)
- *(2025 report, predates Toofan/UDF government)* [Kerala on drugs: school, college students are using MDMA, going to rehabs (ThePrint, April 2025)](https://theprint.in/ground-reports/gods-own-country-is-high-on-mdma-kerala-school-college-students-flood-rehabs/2574881/)

### The big picture


<div class="entry-card" markdown="1">

#### 8. Sequencing: parallel by design, enforcement-weighted in practice

*Ongoing since June 2026*

The government's stated position is explicitly parallel, not sequential —
Chennithala's three-pronged strategy ([entry #6](#rhetoric-vs-execution)) runs enforcement,
identification, and rehabilitation together, and Toofan 2.0 added its
grama-sabha/Kudumbashree community layer and expanded hospital
partnerships alongside continued raids, not after them. In practice,
though, independent reporting shows the actual weighting skews heavily
toward enforcement: arrest and seizure numbers are large, tracked in real
time, and heavily publicized, while the care and rehabilitation side lags
far behind in measured throughput ([entry #3](#toofan-care-capacity)) and isn't reported with
anything like the same rigor. So the honest answer to "which should come
first, or how should they be weighted" is: officially parallel, actually
enforcement-weighted — and that one gap in measurement and
infrastructure build-out is the common root of the targeting
([#5](#peddlers-vs-users)), capacity ([#3](#toofan-care-capacity)–[#4](#custody-access)), and visibility
([#1](#media-visibility)) criticisms above, more than any of
them being a separate, unrelated failure.
